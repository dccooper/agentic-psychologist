# Real concerns about AI
{: .no_toc }

Many psychologists are wary of AI, often for good reasons. This page takes those reasons seriously. For each one: the concern in its strongest form, what's true, a gentle pushback where it's earned, and what this course does about it.

## The four big ones

### Privacy

**The concern.** Anything typed into an AI tool leaves your hands. In clinical work that can mean a confidentiality breach, a legal violation, and real harm to a client.

**What's true.** It's a real risk. What you type, and the files an AI agent reads, are sent to the provider's servers. Removing names doesn't make information safe: dates, places and details can still identify someone.

**What the course does.** One rule, from the first lesson to the last: **never put real patient or research-participant data into it.** Lesson 0.2 is entirely about what never goes into an AI tool, and the capstone ends with a go-live checklist (what data, where it goes, what your institution allows, your red lines) before any real use.

### Accuracy and made-up answers

**The concern.** AI tools state things confidently that aren't true, including fake references, wrong numbers, and invented facts.

**What's true.** They do. Made-up citations have turned up in published papers and court filings.

**What the course does.** Checking is a skill you practice throughout: you review every change before you keep it (1.1), check answers against the source (1.3), and on the lab path you face a deliberate trap where you're asked for "five more references." The agents you build are told to cite only what they were given.

### Environmental impact

**The concern.** Data centers use a lot of energy and water, and AI is driving that growth.

**What's true.** It's a real and growing cost, and it's fair to weigh it.

**Putting it in proportion.** Google [reports about 0.24 Wh of energy and 0.26 mL of water](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference) per typical Gemini chat prompt. An agent like the one in this course does more work per message: it reads your files and carries the whole conversation, so [each message costs many times more](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use) ([one estimate for coding agents](https://simonpcouch.com/blog/2026-01-20-cc-impact/), [another](https://the-decoder.com/ai-agents-use-roughly-600-times-more-energy-than-a-simple-chat-prompt/)). Our rough estimate for the whole course is a few kilowatt-hours at most, in the same range as [streaming a season or two of TV](https://www.carbonbrief.org/factcheck-what-is-the-carbon-footprint-of-streaming-video-on-netflix/), and a few liters of cooling water, less than [one short shower](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2017-01/documents/ws-products-support-statement-showerheads.pdf). That estimate leaves out the water used to generate the electricity, and we'll replace it with real numbers once we've measured how many messages learners actually send.

### "Opting out" versus not knowing

**The concern.** Psychologists shouldn't be using these tools at all, and learning them only speeds things up.

**What's true.** Choosing not to use AI is a legitimate choice.

**Gentle pushback.** These tools are arriving in clinics, labs, insurers and electronic health records whether or not we understand them. Not knowing how they work, where they fail, and what never belongs in them leaves you without a say. Understanding is what makes the choice yours, including the choice to say no.

## More questions

<details markdown="block">
<summary><strong>Bias</strong></summary>

AI reflects the data it was trained on, and so does research. The course builds in checks: on the clinic path you ask who your website assumes it's talking to, and on the lab path you check who's actually in the sample before saying what the findings mean.
</details>

<details markdown="block">
<summary><strong>Losing skills</strong></summary>

If the AI does the work, do you stop being able to? The course is built so it doesn't: the tutor never does your work for you, and you write, edit and decide throughout. The AI drafts; you judge.
</details>

<details markdown="block">
<summary><strong>Jobs and the profession</strong></summary>

Nobody knows exactly how AI will change psychology. The course's bet is that psychologists who understand these tools are better placed to shape how they're used than those who don't.
</details>
