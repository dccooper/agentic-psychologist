Questions skeptics ask

Is it really free?

The course is free and always will be. It runs in Antigravity, Google's AI editor, on its free plan. You need a Google account, and the free plan has weekly usage limits that Google doesn't publish. If you reach one, stop and come back later. Your place is saved. If Antigravity's terms change, we'll update this answer first.

I've never used a code editor. Can I do this?

Yes. That's who it's for. You won't write code. The setup guide shows every screen, and the first lessons are about finding your way around: where the files are, and how to talk to the tutor. The tutor gives one step at a time and checks your work before moving on.

What happens to what I type?

What you type, and the files the AI reads, are sent to Google's AI models so they can reply. During setup, Antigravity asks whether Google may use your interactions to improve its products. That box is checked by default, and you can uncheck it. Either way, this is why the course has one firm rule: never put real patient or research-participant data into it. Everything you practice on is fictional or openly licensed.

Should psychologists be doing this at all?

Choosing not to use AI is a legitimate choice. But these tools are arriving in clinics, labs and electronic health records whether or not we understand them. Knowing how they work, where they fail and what never belongs in them is what lets you set limits, push back and advocate for the people you serve. The course spends as much time on checking AI's work as on using it.

Is the University of Tomorrow real?

No. It's a fictional department where you can practice without risk: the emails, transcripts and messy shared drives are all made up. Any resemblance to your own department's coffee machine is a coincidence.
