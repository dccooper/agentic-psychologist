# Lesson 3B.3b: Vibe Code an Analysis: The Reveal

- **Module:** 3B (lab)
- **Time:** ~25 min
- **Request budget:** ~9 agent requests (estimate; verify in free-tier testing)
- **Last tested:** not yet tested

## Goal

The learner turns their results into a small **dashboard**, then has the agent **fetch the authors' published paper and their R analysis**, and compares the human, peer-reviewed analysis with their agent-made one: same answer? Same exclusions? Same test? They finish with a README so anyone could rerun their work.

## Why it matters to a psychologist

Checking an AI's analysis against expert human work is the most honest test of it there is. Usually you don't get the answer key. Here you do. And the differences matter as much as the agreement: two careful analyses of the same data can make different, defensible choices. That's what "forking paths" means, and why writing your choices down matters.

## Setup

- From 3B.3a: `workspace/lab/analysis/` (`analysis-plan.md`, `sample.md`, `analysis.py`, `results.md`, `analysis-log.md`). **If they're missing**, go back to 3B.3a.
- **The firewall comes down in step 3 of this lesson, not before.** Until then, the same rule as 3B.3a applies.
- **Sources to fetch in step 3** (both open access, CC BY 4.0):
  - The paper's text (XML from PLOS, about 140 KB): `https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0237435&type=manuscript`. It's also readable in a browser at `https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0237435`.
  - The authors' main R analysis from OSF: `https://osf.io/download/27yv8/` (`EFT_Controls.Rmd`, about 27 KB).
  - Save both to `workspace/lab/reveal/`. **To save free-plan usage, don't read the whole XML.** Search it for the abstract, the data-analysis part of the Methods, and the Results.
- **Expected comparison points (for the tutor; read only from step 3 on, and confirm against the fetched files):**
  - **Sample:** Amazon Mechanical Turk workers, N = 254.
  - **Exclusions:** the authors removed **only** the `Manual.Excl` rows (failed writing quality, 52; outside the US, 4), leaving **198**. They **did not** exclude `AC.PassBoth` = "No." In the Rmd, they also note the total excluding the 4 non-US participants as 250, while the abstract says N = 254. Worth noticing.
  - **Test:** they checked normality, found `AUC` wasn't normal, and used **beta regression** (`betareg(AUC ~ Group)`, suited to outcomes between 0 and 1), with an omnibus chi-square test and **pairwise comparisons adjusted with FDR**. Then they removed a few overly influential points (Cook's distance; large residuals) and reran it: *"the same nominal results (just bigger effects)."*
  - **Finding (abstract):** EFT reduced discounting relative to both HIT and the standard control (ERT). The control groups weren't equivalent to each other.
  - **Likely learner differences:** an ANOVA or a Kruskal–Wallis test instead of beta regression; a different decision on attention checks; no correction for multiple comparisons; no influence diagnostics. None of these is automatically "wrong." The question is whether the conclusion holds.

## Steps

1. **What should the dashboard show?** Dr. Lund wants "a dashboard like that paper." Dr. Bello's rule: decide what it needs to *show* first. Ask: *"If Dr. Lund only looks at this for 30 seconds, what must he see?"* Help them pick three or four things: the question, group sizes after exclusions, `AUC` by group (a chart that shows the spread, not just the averages), the main result in one honest sentence, and **who the sample is**.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** they've chosen the contents, including the sample caveat.

2. **Build it.** Have them ask the AI for a script, `workspace/lab/analysis/make_dashboard.py`, that builds `workspace/lab/analysis/dashboard.html`: one file, charts included, **no outside scripts or web fonts**, using their results and their honest summary from 3B.3a. The AI runs it (explained, approved). They open the dashboard in their browser and refine one thing.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** `dashboard.html` exists, opens, shows the contents they chose, and states who the sample is. **Pause point.**

3. **The reveal: fetch the authors' work.** Now the firewall comes down. Have them ask the agent to download the two sources in Setup into `workspace/lab/reveal/`. **Before approving, they read each command** (2.4): it downloads one file from a named site into the folder they chose, and runs nothing. If downloading fails, they can open the links in a browser and save the files there themselves.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** both files are in `workspace/lab/reveal/`.

4. **Compare.** Have them ask the agent to build a comparison table in `workspace/lab/reveal/comparison.md`, **reading only the fetched files and their own analysis**: rows for **sample size after exclusions, exclusion rules, attention checks, the test used and why, multiple-comparison correction, outlier handling, the main result, and the direction of the effect**; columns for **ours** and **the authors'**. They review it, checking at least two rows against the sources themselves.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** compare with the expected points in Setup. The table should show the authors kept the attention-check "No" rows (198), used beta regression with FDR-adjusted comparisons, and found EFT reduced discounting vs. both controls.

5. **Who was right?** A short conversation, about three minutes. Ask: *"Did you reach the same conclusion? Where did you differ, and does each difference matter?"* Draw out:
   - why a 0–1 outcome like `AUC` suits beta regression better than an ANOVA (and why an ANOVA might still give the same answer);
   - that the attention-check decision was a **fork**: both choices are defensible; the important thing was writing it down first;
   - that agreeing with the authors doesn't prove the agent was right, and disagreeing doesn't prove it wrong. **Checking is how you find out.**
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** they can name one place they matched the authors and one place they differed, and say whether the difference changed the conclusion. They add this to `analysis-log.md`.

6. **Make it rerunnable.** Have them ask the AI to write `workspace/lab/analysis/README.md`: what the question was, where the data comes from (with the CC BY credit from `data/README.md`), how to install what's needed, how to run the scripts in order, and where the decisions are recorded. They review it. *Why:* reproducibility means someone else, or you in six months, can get the same answer.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** read the README. It has the question, the data credit, the install steps, and the run order.

7. **Name the moment.** Two sentences: *"You ran a real analysis with an AI agent, then checked it against peer-reviewed human work, and you can explain every place they agree and differ. Fast analysis is only worth something when you can explain it, reproduce it, and check it, and now you've done all three."* **End of the Lab path's main lessons.**

## Done when

`dashboard.html` exists and states who the sample is; both sources are in `workspace/lab/reveal/`; `comparison.md` compares at least the sample, exclusions, test, and result; the learner has added their "same and different" note to the log; and `README.md` explains how to rerun everything.

## Common sticking points

- **The download is blocked or fails** → open the two links in a browser and save the files into `workspace/lab/reveal/` by hand. The PLOS article can also be read online at the DOI link.
- **The XML is huge and slow** → have the agent search it for "Data analysis," "Results," and the abstract instead of reading all of it.
- **Their result disagrees with the authors'** → great lesson material. Find the fork: exclusions? The test? Outliers? Rerun with the authors' choice as an **exploratory** check, and log it.
- **They feel the agent "cheated"** (it seemed to know the answer) → possible: the paper is public, and models train on public text. That's why the code and the log matter. Their analysis is checkable either way.
- **They want to publish the dashboard** → it's built on CC BY data, so they can, with credit. Publishing comes in Graduation (G.2).

## Avoid

- Don't reveal anything from the paper or the Rmd before step 3.
- Don't declare a winner. Help them reason about the forks.
- Don't read the full XML in one go (free-plan usage).
