# Lesson 3A.3b: Vibe Code a Clinic Website: Refine and Check

- **Module:** 3A (clinic)
- **Time:** ~20 min
- **Request budget:** ~8 agent requests (estimate; verify in free-tier testing)
- **Last tested:** Antigravity 2.5.5, QA pass 4: passed. Revised from the tester's notes; retest in the final end-to-end run.

## Goal

The learner refines the site in a **describe → build → look** loop, directing the AI in their own words. They have the AI explain a piece of the code and edit it by hand, then run four checks: **professional ethics** (advertising), **accessibility**, **who the page is for**, and **before you publish** (security and privacy).

## Why it matters to a psychologist

A clinic website is a public statement by psychologists, so the profession's ethics rules apply to it. AI writes marketing copy fluently, including the kinds of claims and testimonials psychologists aren't supposed to use. Knowing what to check for is professional judgment that AI doesn't replace.

## Setup

- `workspace/clinic/website/index.html` (from 3A.3a). **If it's missing**, go back to 3A.3a.
- `world/artifacts/daybreak-facts.md` and `world/artifacts/referral-partners.md` (for the audience check: the Community Health Collective serves many Spanish-speaking families)
- **The ethics standard (US):** the APA *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct*, **Standard 5, Advertising and Other Public Statements**, especially **5.01** (no false, deceptive, or misleading statements, including about training and credentials) and **5.05** (psychologists don't solicit testimonials from current therapy clients, or from others vulnerable to undue influence; Daybreak's facts sheet rules out testimonials entirely). The full code is free on apa.org. **For educators outside the US:** swap in your own code (e.g., the BPS, CPA, or APS code), and keep the same exercise.
- **Expected ethics findings (for the tutor):** no superlatives ("best"), no outcome guarantees ("proven to cure"), no testimonials, trainee and supervision status stated clearly, and nothing that implies services the facts sheet doesn't list (e.g., crisis care).
- **Expected "before you publish" findings (for the tutor):** no passwords or secrets in the file (the old website password from the blue binder must not appear); no client or personal information; no HTML comments (notes in the code that don't show on the page, but anyone can read with **View Source**) holding anything private; no outside scripts or trackers (no `<script src="http…">`); the phone is **(555) 010-0142** and the address is Room 104; any links work.
- **The learner drives.** This is the last main lesson of the path. Coach, don't guide: ask *"What do you want to change?"* and let them direct the AI in their own words, typed or spoken. Offer ideas only if they're stuck.

## UI this lesson depends on

See `lessons/SCREEN_GUIDE.md`: reviewing changes; editing a file by hand; refreshing the browser; **View Source** in the browser (⚠️ usually right-click the page → **View page source**, or Ctrl+U). If the page's tab is closed, they ask the AI to open it again (the same command as 3A.3a, explained first) or use **Reveal in File Explorer** / **Reveal in Finder**. ⚠️ *Optional:* pasting a screenshot into the agent panel (unverified in Antigravity).

## Steps

1. **The loop.** Have them open the page in the browser again. Ask: *"What two things do you want to change?"* Let them choose (layout, colors, wording, order). For each one: they describe it to the AI in their own words, review the change, **refresh the browser**, and look. *Why:* describe → build → look is the whole of vibe coding. Small steps are easier to check.
   - *Optional:* if they can paste a screenshot into the agent panel, they can point at what they mean. Note whether it works in `progress.md`.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** two changes made and seen in the browser.
   - **Quick knowledge check:** *"The AI just changed what you asked for. What do you check before accepting?"* (Expected: the **whole** change, not just the part you asked for, because the AI sometimes changes other things too. That's review before accept, from 1.1.)

2. **Look under the hood.** Have them ask the AI to explain, in plain language, how **one part** of the page works (the header, or where the colors are set). Then have them change one small thing **by hand** in `index.html`, such as a color or a heading's wording, save, and refresh. *Why:* the code isn't magic either. It's text you can read and edit, like `AGENTS.md`.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** they made a hand edit to the code and saw it in the browser.
   - **Pause point.**

3. **Ethics check.** Explain in two sentences that a clinic website is a public statement, so APA Ethics Code Standard 5 applies (see Setup; they can read it on apa.org). Have them ask the AI to check the page against **5.01** and **5.05** and against the "not to be included" line in `daybreak-facts.md`, and **list** any problems (not fix them yet). Then they decide what to change, and tell the AI.
   - **Say plainly:** the AI's check is a first pass, not a ruling. They're the professional. For a real practice, check your licensing board's rules too.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** read `index.html` and compare with the expected findings in Setup. In particular: no testimonials, no "best" or "cure," and the trainee/supervision statement is clear.

4. **Accessibility check.** Have them ask the AI to check the page for basic accessibility: headings in order, **alt text** (a short description of an image, read aloud by screen readers) on any images, enough **color contrast**, readable text size, clear link text, and whether it works on a phone screen. Then have them **narrow the browser window** to phone width and look. They fix what they agree with.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** they fixed at least one accessibility issue and checked the narrow view.

5. **Who is this for?** Ask: *"Who does this page assume it's talking to? Who might read it and feel it isn't for them?"* Let them think first. Prompts only if needed: someone who's never heard "psychological assessment," a parent worried about cost, a teenager, a Spanish-speaking family referred by the Community Health Collective. Have them make **one change** for a reader they'd missed.
   - If they add Spanish text: it must match the facts ("available on some days; call to ask"), and **AI translations should be checked by a fluent speaker** before going live.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** they named a reader the page didn't serve well and made one change for them.

6. **Before you publish.** Publishing itself comes in Graduation (G.2). Checking comes first. One line on why: *"Once it's on the web, assume it's copied and kept forever."* Have them ask the AI to check `index.html` for each of these and list what it finds:
   - no secrets or passwords anywhere in the file;
   - no client or personal information;
   - nothing in hidden comments (notes in the code that visitors don't see on the page, but anyone can read) that they wouldn't want public;
   - no outside scripts or trackers;
   - the contact details are the ones meant to be public;
   - every link works.

   Then they check two things **themselves**: the phone number and address against `daybreak-facts.md`, and a quick look at **View Source** in the browser. *Why:* check against the source, not the AI's word.
   - ✅ **Checkpoint:** read `index.html` and compare with the expected "before you publish" findings in Setup. Anything found is fixed.

7. **Name the moment.** Two sentences: *"You built a website by describing it, and then did the part only a psychologist could: checked it against your profession's ethics, made it usable for more people, asked who it leaves out, and made sure it's safe to share. Building is fast now; judgment is the job."* Publishing it comes in Graduation (G.2).

## Done when

The page has two learner-chosen refinements and a hand edit; it passes the ethics check (no testimonials, no guarantees or superlatives, trainee status clear); at least one accessibility fix is made; one change serves a reader they'd missed; and it passes the before-you-publish check (no secrets, no personal information, no private comments, no outside scripts, correct public contact details, working links).

## Common sticking points

- **The AI adds new marketing claims while fixing something else** ("compassionate, evidence-based excellence") → check every change, not just the one you asked for. It's 1.1's review habit.
- **They disagree with the AI's ethics read** → good. Talk it through. Their judgment wins. The Ethics Code itself is free to read on apa.org.
- **The page looks broken after a hand edit** → Ctrl+Z, save, refresh. Or ask the AI what went wrong. Breaking it and fixing it is how people learn code.
- **They closed the browser tab** → ask the AI to open the page again (it explains the command first), or use the Reveal fallback.
- **"There's nothing secret in it, why check?"** → it's a habit for every page they'll ever publish. Today it takes a minute. Later, the file might have a draft note or an old password in a comment.
- **They're not in the US** → use their own code of ethics. The exercise is the same.

## Avoid

- Don't add client stories or quotes, even as "examples" of what not to do.
- Don't present the AI's ethics or security check as authoritative.
- Don't offer change ideas before they've chosen their own. They drive; you coach.
- Don't tell them to type `/next-step` between steps. Just ask *"Ready to move on?"*
- Don't publish anything. Don't install anything.
